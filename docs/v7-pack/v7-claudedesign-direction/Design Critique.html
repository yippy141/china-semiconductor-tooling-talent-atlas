M0 Memorandum  ·  Home page hierarchy

Notes on the Atlas home.

The product reads as a serious public-source monitor — its restraint, its source-checked badges, its refusal to rank firms — but the home page still presents that material with the visual habits of a SaaS dashboard. Six notes on hierarchy, with a redesigned first two scrolls.

Reviewed  home, brief, four dossiers Reference  FT / Economist / McKinsey Quarterly Scope  Above the fold + style direction
01   Hierarchy

The home is four sections doing the same job.

Reading

A reader landing on the page meets the headline question — Can China staff its chip-tooling push? — and then, within two scrolls, encounters the same three findings restated in three different layouts: a four-card Analyst Brief, a three-card Findings row, and a comparator frame in the body. Each block carries a fresh eyebrow (Analyst Brief · Findings · Comparator Frame · Listed-Company Disclosure), which gives every block equal billing and erases the lede.

The deeper problem is that the home asks the reader to navigate rather than read. The hero offers three primary CTAs of identical weight; the right column carries a Core Question pull-out that competes with the headline; the source counts (43, 143, 4, 4) — which are the single most important credibility signal on the page — are buried between Analyst Brief and Findings as a quiet strip.

  • One lede, restated three times.

    Sections 01 (Analyst Brief), 02 (Findings) and the dossier comparator all carry the same three takeaways — STEM is the wrong denominator, filings are the best window, customer-site support is invisible. Pick one section to deliver the argument and let the others do supporting work.

  • The hero scatters attention.

    Three CTAs of equal weight, plus a Core Question pull-out, plus a beta caveat, plus the title — the eye has nowhere to land. A McKinsey or FT opener has one primary entry (Read the brief) and lets a single exhibit do the talking on the right.

  • Equal cards flatten the page.

    White rounded-corner cards on a light gray field are SaaS dashboard grammar. A brief has rules, not cards: hairlines, marginalia, drop caps, indented standfirsts. Cards say module; rules say publication.

  • Eyebrow inflation.

    Almost every block opens with its own uppercase kicker. When everything is labelled, labels stop carrying any meaning. Reserve eyebrows for section openers; let in-section blocks rely on the section's existing frame.

  • The credibility numbers are hidden.

    43 / 143 / 4 / 4 is the answer to “why should I trust this?” It belongs near the standfirst, not buried six blocks down as a thin strip beside Findings.

  • The caveat is in the wrong place.

    “Beta public-source monitor. Counts show source coverage, not workforce size.” is essential, but placing it under the headline dilutes the lede. Move it to a methodology note at the foot of the hero — visible, but not in headline weight.

02   Layout

A stronger opening — two scrolls, one argument.

Intent

The first scroll should answer three questions before the reader's thumb leaves the trackpad: what is this, what is its headline claim, and can I trust it. The second scroll should deliver the finding once — in prose, not in cards — and only then promote the source counts as the credibility band underneath.

The mockup below uses real text and figures from the brief and dossiers. Visual mode is set against the current home: serif throughout, hairlines instead of card chrome, a single amber accent, one primary action.

01   The argument

The public record shows scale
before customer-site depth.

Most coverage of China's chip-tooling push tracks the same scoreboard — a new etch platform from Shanghai, a fresh deposition tool out of Shenyang. The launches are genuine. They describe only the first half of what a foundry needs. The vendor-side question is not whether the box ships, but whether the firm shipping it can install, tune, match and recover the tool inside a customer's ramp. The public record now shows enough to say where that harder problem is being staffed and where it is still being skipped.

01

Broad STEM totals are the wrong denominator.

Etch, deposition, metrology and lithography-adjacent tools each draw on a different mix of plasma, thin-film, optical and controls talent. A national graduate total collapses those mixes into one number.

02

Listed-firm filings are the clearest workforce window.

AMEC, ACM Research Shanghai, NAURA and Piotech disclose more usable detail than education statistics — R&D scale, technical staff, degree mix, after-sales categories.

03

Customer-site support is the hardest layer to see.

Product pages show ambition; filings show R&D scale. Whether a firm can install, tune and recover a tool inside a customer fab — the package question — is barely disclosed.

What changed

The hero loses two CTAs and the right-column pull-out. In their place, a single primary action and an immediate exhibit — the four anchor firms with one disclosed figure each. The figures arrive before the argument, so the reader knows what they are about to be told something about.

The second scroll consolidates the four-card Analyst Brief and the three-card Findings into a single argument page: one drop-cap paragraph that carries the case, three subordinate findings as numbered prose, and the credibility band (43 / 143 / 4 / 4) promoted to the foot of the scroll where it belongs.

The current dark City signal map and the firm dossier exhibit remain — they are the strongest pieces on the page. Place them third and fourth, after the argument has been made.

03   Style

Tailwind-level direction.

Voice

The aesthetic register to aim for is closer to an FT special report or a McKinsey Quarterly piece than to a SaaS dashboard. That means: serif throughout, hairlines and rules instead of card chrome, one accent at most, monospace reserved for IDs and figures, generous margins, and column-grid alignment so the page reads like printed pages rather than a stack of widgets.

04   Subtract

What to remove.

  • The Core Question pull-out in the hero.

    It restates the headline. A pull-quote that paraphrases the headline weakens both. Put the standfirst where the pull-out is; move the exhibit there instead.

  • Two of the three hero CTAs.

    Keep Read the brief as the single primary action. Demote Open firm dossiers and Inspect source rows to a quiet text link or the masthead nav.

  • Section 01 Analyst Brief's four-card grid — or section 02 Findings. Not both.

    They carry the same content in different formats. The proposal in §02 above merges them into a single argument scroll with prose lede and numbered subordinate findings.

  • White rounded-corner cards on light-gray background.

    Replace every card grid with rule-bounded regions. Cards are SaaS; rules are editorial. Keep card containers only inside the dark City signal exhibit, where they read as data-viz UI rather than chrome.

  • The per-block eyebrows.

    LISTED-COMPANY DISCLOSURE, COMPARATOR FRAME, FIRM WORKFORCE CHART, PUBLIC PRODUCT RECORD — the page is shouting. Keep an eyebrow only on the section opener.

  • The beta caveat in headline weight.

    It's important; it isn't the lede. Move to a methodology line under the hero CTA in muted Plex Sans, 10–11px, tracked.

  • The full nav row at the very top (HOME / FIRMS / EXPLORER) inside the article column.

    It floats unattached. Pin it to a thin masthead with the publication name, and treat it as chrome, not content.

05   Keep

What not to change.

  • The editorial restraint.

    No rankings, no scores, no composite indices, no leaderboards. This is the single feature that distinguishes the product from every other China-tech tracker; protect it.

  • The dark City signal map exhibit.

    This is the strongest piece on the page. Reverse-out section, gridded background, restrained yellow highlight on the active node, paired with an inspector that names what the size means and what it doesn't. Use it as the visual template for any future exhibit.

  • The denominator discipline.

    Every figure in the dossiers labels its denominator and refuses to roll up across categories. Keep that habit visible in the exhibits; it's the page's intellectual signature.

  • The Source checked badge and the source-row ID treatment.

    The green check + monospace source-id pattern (CN_FILING_AMEC_2025) reads as serious archival metadata. Keep it; reuse it consistently.

  • The comparator frame's Compare on / Do not compare on list.

    This is the most McKinsey-feeling piece of writing on the site. It belongs in the brief, not in a competing home section, but the framing itself is exactly right.

  • The bilingual firm names.

    AMEC / 中微公司, Piotech / 拓荆科技. Quiet, factual, signals primary-source contact. Keep at every dossier head.

06   Notes

Other observations.

  • Give the brief a date and an issue number.

    Public-source monitors gain credibility by versioning visibly. Issue 04 · May 24, 2026 tells the reader the work is maintained on a cadence. april 17 (v1) in the tab title is doing this work in the wrong place.

  • Lead with an exhibit, not with cards.

    The four anchor firms each have one disclosed figure that is interesting on its own (AMEC's 52.24% R&D share, ACM's 49.42% technical-staff share, NAURA's 21,101, Piotech's 638). A four-row exhibit beside the headline does more work than the current Core Question pull-out.

  • Section numbers are doing two jobs. Pick one.

    Right now 01 ANALYST BRIEF reads both as a chapter marker and as an eyebrow. Promote it (§ 01 in section heads, large, with a hair-rule above) or drop it. The half-state is what reads as SaaS.

  • Promote the discipline-to-segment matrix earlier.

    It is the cleverest piece of the whole atlas. Inside the home it currently sits below the dossier exhibit. Either pull it into the argument section as the second exhibit, or give it its own section header (“Why STEM totals miss”) and let the matrix stand alone.

  • Resist visualising what the data can't carry.

    The footprint grid (Chinese toolmakers to watch) is on the edge. CORE SOURCE filled cells next to NO CURRENT RECORD dashed cells look like a scorecard to a casual reader, even though the captions say otherwise. Consider re-rendering as a coverage matrix (dots scaled to row-count) so the visual cue matches the analytical one.

  • One typeface change does most of the work.

    The single highest-leverage change is moving body and headlines to an editorial serif. The page is currently set in a default neo-grotesque sans that signals web product. A serif (Source Serif 4, Tiempos, Lyon, GT Sectra — the family doesn't matter; the register does) repositions the entire artefact toward publication.

  • The dossier page header has the right idea — promote it home.

    Big Latin name, smaller Hanzi, a single-line standfirst, segment chips, hairline rule, then the body in two columns (Analyst Read / Boundary Condition). That's the exact grammar the home hero should adopt.

The product is doing the difficult half right: it has refused to invent a league table and built a record of denominators instead. The home page just hasn't caught up to the seriousness of the work behind it. Summary
