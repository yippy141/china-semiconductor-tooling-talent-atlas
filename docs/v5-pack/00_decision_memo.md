# Decision memo

## Diagnosis

The project has a good research base and a useful codebase. The weak point is the public story.

A reader lands on the site and sees dataset counts, evidence types, caveats, and instructions. That proves the dataset exists. It does not make clear why a corporate strategy team, a policy shop, or a data journalist should care.

The project should stop asking readers to understand the atlas first. It should answer a real question first.

## New product question

> Are Chinese semiconductor-equipment firms building the people and support organizations needed to make domestic tools work in customer fabs?

That question is concrete. It is relevant to toolmakers, investors, policymakers, and analysts. It also fits the evidence you already have.

## New public title

Use this on the homepage:

> Can China Staff Its Chip-Tooling Push?

Use this as the deck:

> Public filings and company records show Chinese toolmakers adding R&D scale and product breadth. The harder test is whether they are building field engineers, service teams, calibration routines, and customer-support capacity.

Keep the project brand in the nav or footer:

> China Semiconductor Tooling Talent Atlas

## What the site is for

The site is a public-source brief and reference tool for monitoring Chinese semiconductor-equipment firms.

It helps users do three things:

1. Understand which skills are needed for etch, deposition, metrology, and lithography-adjacent tools.
2. See what Chinese firms publicly disclose about workforce scale, product families, and support functions.
3. Click from article claims into firm pages, segment pages, source records, and evidence rows.

## What the site is not for

Do not make the homepage lead with these points. Put them in methodology and footer text.

- It is not a labor-market census.
- It is not a ranking.
- It is not a capability benchmark.
- It does not estimate exact headcount by specialty.

## What to cut from the homepage

Cut or demote these sections:

- Dataset statistics as the first major section.
- Audience implications cards.
- Monitoring questions cards if they read like instructions.
- Repeated caveats in headings.
- Any title that says `X, not Y`.

Keep these, but put them later:

- Evidence ladder.
- Methodology link.
- Source ledger link.
- Full explorer.

## What to keep front and center

The homepage should lead with:

1. The article thesis.
2. A workforce chart for AMEC, ACM Research Shanghai, and NAURA.
3. A firm footprint grid including Piotech, Jingce, SMEE, and BEIM.
4. A discipline-to-segment matrix.
5. A short section on what public records miss: customer-site support and tacit process knowledge.
6. Calls to click into firm dossiers and segment briefs.

## The one-sentence pitch

> I built a public-source brief and monitoring prototype that tracks whether Chinese semiconductor-equipment firms are building the workforce and support capacity needed to turn product claims into usable fab tools.
